## Overview

I helped the Driver Interface subteam repair and finish the driver's seat for our Formula SAE car.
The seat is a custom composite shell — fiberglass over a foam mold, finished with an outer layer of
carbon fiber.

During the original build, the lap belt position was misjudged and a hole was cut into the fiberglass
shell for the belt to pass through. In the final design the lap belt didn't need to go through the
seat at all; it needed to run over the seat, over a shallow curve. I reshaped the foam mold so the
hole would close flat and molded that curve beside it, then did the fiberglass layup that filled the
hole. I also laid up a full carbon fiber layer over the entire seat myself, vacuum bagged both
layups, and sanded the seat to finish it before it was installed in the car.

## The problem

The seat is one of the most important parts of the driver interface: it has to hold the driver
securely, fit their body, and work with the safety harness. Because the lap belt position was
misjudged when the seat was made, the shell had a hole in the wrong place.

A hole the belt doesn't use leaves a weak spot in the shell, creates an edge that could catch or wear
the belt, and makes the seat harder to finish cleanly. The lap belt needed to run over the seat
surface instead of passing through it.

## What I did

### Reshaping the mold

First we sanded down the foam mold the Driver Interface subteam had used to make the seat. I
reshaped the mold where the hole had been so the new layup would close the hole flat, flush with the
surrounding shell. Outside the hole, I molded a curve into the foam for the lap belt to run over.
Because fiberglass takes the shape of the mold underneath it, the layup formed both directly: a flat
patch over the old hole, and the curve for the belt.

### Fiberglass patch

Using a wet layup, I wet out the fiberglass cloth with epoxy by hand and built up two to three plies
over the hole. I cut each ply to overlap past the edges of the hole and worked it into the curved
surface of the seat, so the patch followed the mold and bonded to the surrounding shell. The
hole was covered from both the outside and the inside, so the patch is supported on both faces of the
shell rather than only covering the opening from one side.

### Vacuum bagging

I vacuum bagged the fiberglass layup while it cured. The layup was covered with breather fabric,
sealed inside a plastic bag with sealant tape, and a vacuum pump pulled the air out. With the air
gone, atmospheric pressure presses the plies firmly against the mold, squeezes out trapped air and
excess resin, and helps the layers bond tightly with fewer voids. The breather gives the air an even
path out across the whole part and soaks up excess resin, so the vacuum pulls on the entire surface
instead of sealing off in spots.

### Carbon fiber layer

After the fiberglass cured, I sanded the seat smooth to give the next layer a good surface to bond
to. I then laid up one ply of carbon fiber over the entire seat, also as a wet layup with epoxy, and
vacuum bagged it the same way — this time sealing the whole seat inside the bag. Because the seat is
a complex curved shape, the bag needs extra folds (pleats) so the film can stretch into the curves
without tearing or bridging over them when the vacuum is pulled. After curing, I sanded the seat
again to clean up the surface and edges for the final finish.

## Results

The hole is closed flat, and the patched area is now part of the seat's continuous shell, with a
molded curve beside it for the lap belt. The finished seat has a single carbon fiber layer over the
whole fiberglass shell and was installed in the car with the lap belt routed over that curve, as
intended.

## What I learned

This project gave me hands-on composites experience: wet layups with fiberglass and carbon fiber on a
curved part, wetting out cloth with epoxy, and vacuum bagging — including sealing the bag around a
complex shape like a seat. I learned how shaping the mold controls the shape of the finished part,
and why a patch needs enough overlap with the surrounding material to bond and carry load.

Surface preparation mattered at every stage: sanding the mold before the layup, between the
fiberglass and carbon fiber, and after the final layer all affected how well the layers bonded and
how clean the finished part looked.

It also showed me how a small positioning mistake early in a design can create extra manufacturing
work later — and why it's important to confirm interfaces between subsystems, like where a harness
mounts and runs, before a part is made.
